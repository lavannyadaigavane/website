the paradox of choice

I wanted each and every one of them, but choosing one meant losing all the rest.

4 november 2026

Okay, so I’ve been thinking about how we’re never really satisfied with our choices.

And I mean, look at us. We can spend twenty minutes deciding what to eat, finally order something, and then regret not ordering the other thing. It’s almost impressive how quickly we can turn a perfectly good decision into a questionable one.

But I think this happens with much bigger things too.

There’s a concept called the Paradox of Choice, which Barry Schwartz wrote a whole book about. The idea is that more options don’t actually make us happier. Past a certain point they just make choosing harder, and they make whatever we end up picking feel slightly worse afterwards.

The easiest way to see it is Netflix. You sit down with a free evening and nothing at all in the way. Forty minutes later you have scrolled past about two hundred things, watched four trailers, and put on something you have already seen twice. Not because there was nothing good on. Because there was too much good on, and you only get the one evening.

And that’s the easy version of this, because you can always just watch the other thing tomorrow.

Every now and then, I wonder what my life would look like if I’d made just one decision differently. What if I’d chosen a different college? Taken a different opportunity? Said yes to something I turned down? Would I have met the same people? Would I even recognise the person I’d become?

Life can only be understood backwards; but it must be lived forwards.
Søren Kierkegaard

It reminds me of The Midnight Library, and of Everything Everywhere All at Once. Both leave you with the same question: how many versions of ourselves could we have been?

I think we romanticise those versions a little too much. In my imagination, they are all doing wonderfully.

But I never picture them having a bad day. I don’t imagine their disappointments, their difficult friendships or the things they wish they’d done differently.

I only see the highlights of lives that don’t even exist.

And isn’t that a little unfair to the life I’m actually living?

The other strange bit is that she wouldn’t be me. The version who moved cities isn’t sitting in her flat feeling envious of this one. She doesn’t exist, and if she did, she’d have her own list of lives she didn’t pick. The only person doing any of this comparing is the one who ended up here.

There’s another thing I’ve noticed. We often give so much importance to big decisions that we forget how much randomness contributes to our stories. A missed bus, an unexpected invitation, sitting next to a particular person or deciding to take a different route home. Sometimes, the smallest things end up becoming part of our favourite memories.

Which makes me wonder how many important moments in our lives happened without us knowing they were important.

Of course, not every decision works out. Some choices leave us disappointed, and some make us question ourselves for a long time. But I don’t think our past selves deserve all that criticism. They made decisions without having access to the information we have today. It’s easy to judge a choice when you already know how the story ends.

Perhaps that’s the difficult part of growing up: accepting that we cannot experience every possibility.

There will always be a place we didn’t visit, a person we didn’t meet, a career we didn’t pursue or a completely different life we could have imagined. And I suppose that’s just how being human works. We have limited time, but an almost unlimited ability to imagine what we could do with it.

Apparently there are two ways of making a decision. You can go looking for the best possible option, or you can work out what would be good enough and take the first thing that clears it. People in the first group tend to end up with genuinely better outcomes and feel worse about them. Which is, I think, the most annoying fact I have ever read.

I’ve started wondering whether the real skill isn’t making the perfect choice, but learning how to live with the one we’ve made.

Not because it’s necessarily the best one. Not because everything will magically work out. But because constantly comparing your life to an imaginary alternative is a rather exhausting way to live.

Maybe the other option wasn’t better. Maybe it was just different.

And perhaps somewhere, in another version of the story, there’s someone wondering what would’ve happened if they’d made the choices you made.

Anyway, I don’t have a grand conclusion to this. I’m still someone who takes ages to decide what to order and occasionally wonders about completely unnecessary what-ifs.

But I do hope I learn to spend a little less time imagining the lives I could have lived and a little more time being curious about the one that’s unfolding.

P.S. If you still can’t decide what to eat after reading this, please just order the food.

it was just different.
